## Neutral close-read staging notes

### S01E01 — Strange New Worlds

- Pike is depicted withdrawing from command while carrying a remembered/experienced vision of his future injury. His description establishes his present belief and recollection of that experience; it is not silently promoted into an infallible statement that the future cannot change.
- Kiley 279's warp-signature event is investigated as a technological-development trigger, but the Enterprise crew discovers a warp bomb rather than an ordinary propulsion system. The episode attributes the breakthrough to observations of the earlier Discovery-era battle and subsequent reverse engineering.
- Pike chooses overt intervention after concluding that Starfleet activity already contaminated Kiley's development. His decision conflicts with the existing noninterference rule rather than simply implementing it.
- Pike uses a presentation of Earth's destructive history to pressure Kiley's political factions toward another path. The episode depicts this as intentional persuasion under crisis conditions; it should not be flattened into proof that his intervention was institutionally or ethically uncontested.
- Starfleet's aftermath discussion preserves institutional ambiguity: Pike violated General Order 1, April treats the prior contamination as material context, and the rule is subsequently associated with the name `Prime Directive`.
- La'An recounts surviving a Gorn attack and being rescued by Una. That material is testimony about traumatic history, not an omniscient replay of the off-screen events.

### S01E02 — Children of the Comet

- Uhura explains that Starfleet was not originally a settled life goal for her and relates family loss and later dislocation. Those biographical details are testimony from Uhura within the episode.
- The crew identifies an extinction-level comet threat to pre-warp Persephone III and intervenes. A group calling themselves the Shepherds asserts that the comet, M'hanit, is an ancient arbiter following a preordained path. Their religious/cultural account remains testimony unless separately supported.
- The structure within the comet responds to musical harmonics, and Uhura's linguistic and musical analysis becomes causally important to the crew's survival and solution.
- The Enterprise uses an indirect maneuver to alter the comet's course while avoiding an escalating conflict with the Shepherds. Afterward, material shed from the comet changes atmospheric conditions on Persephone III in a way Spock reports as increasing long-term habitability.
- The comet had encoded information that the crew later interprets as anticipating elements of the encounter. The episode explicitly leaves the implication unresolved. The record should preserve both possible foreknowledge and the possibility that the crew is interpreting an ambiguous message after participating in the event.
- Pike and Una use that uncertainty to challenge a deterministic reading of Pike's future vision. The scene supports underdetermination, not proof that Pike's future either must or must not occur.

### S01E04 — Memento Mori

- Starfleet's Remembrance Day provides an institutional memorial frame. La'An declines the commemorative pin and rejects pressure to process her history in the manner others propose. The episode supports her refusal and stated reasons without authorizing a clinical diagnosis.
- The Finibus III relief mission becomes a Gorn ambush. La'An identifies the attackers through tactical pattern recognition based on her prior experience, and Pike relies on that knowledge while still making his own command decisions.
- La'An repeatedly describes the Gorn in species-essentialist moral terms. Those statements are trauma-shaped character testimony, not objective proof that every Gorn is intrinsically evil or reducible to predation.
- A Spock-La'An mind meld surfaces traumatic recollections including her family's captivity and her brother's sacrifice. The episode frames the material as memory reached through a mind meld and explicitly depicts trauma/subconscious resistance; it must not be rewritten as neutral camera access to historical events.
- The Enterprise crew infers Gorn communication/tactical behavior and uses deception against the pursuing ships. The demonstrated coordination, signaling, and tactical adaptation are counterevidence to any simplistic coding of the Gorn as mindless monsters.
- Seven Enterprise crew deaths are recorded by La'An at the end of the episode. The survival outcome follows a multi-person technical/tactical chain involving command, piloting, engineering, science, security, and medical constraints rather than a single heroic cause.

### S01E06 — Lift Us Where Suffering Cannot Reach

- Pike intervenes when a non-Federation shuttle requests help and later insists on investigating the attacking vessel after it fires on Enterprise. Majalan officials simultaneously assert political autonomy and limits on outside involvement.
- The First Servant is described as a child chosen by lottery and trained for a sacred civic role. Majalan statements about the role initially conceal the mechanism and consequences of `ascension`; early religious/political explanations therefore require later evidentiary revision.
- Majalan quantum-biological medicine is depicted as substantially beyond Enterprise medical capability. M'Benga explores its possible relevance to Rukiya, while Gamal states that Majalan law forbids sharing the technology with unaffiliated peoples.
- Uhura's linguistic analysis establishes a connection between Prospect VII and Majalis that contradicts the earlier presentation of Prospect VII as merely an alien colony. This is explicit counterevidence to Alora's initial framing.
- Gamal's apparent kidnapping is later revealed as an attempt to prevent his son's ascension. His conduct violates both Majalan expectations and Federation rules aboard Enterprise, but his motive is depicted as protecting the child rather than seeking ransom or political gain.
- The ascension ceremony depicts the child being physically connected to an inherited machine after being raised to regard the sacrifice as freely chosen. Alora later states that the machine requires a child's neural network, causes suffering, and has resisted centuries of attempts to find an alternative. Her account explains Majalan belief and practice; the episode does not independently verify every historical claim she makes about the machine's origins.
- Pike rejects the practice and intends to report it, while Alora argues that Federation prosperity also coexists with child suffering and emphasizes Majalis's lack of Federation jurisdiction. The episode presents a direct ethical conflict rather than resolving the comparison into a neutral factual equivalence.
- Gamal changes his view of Prospect VII after its people help him try to save his son and chooses to assist them in preventing a future sacrifice. His later offer of medical theory to M'Benga is a distinct consequence and should not be treated as retrospective endorsement of Majalan institutions.

### S01E07 — The Serene Squall

- Pike enters non-Federation space before Starfleet approval can arrive because he believes alleged colonists face enslavement. The episode depicts a deliberate command choice under urgency, not a claim that regulations permitted the action without tension.
- The person presenting as Dr. Aspen offers Spock advice against rigid human/Vulcan identity categories. That dialogue affects the identity theme, but the speaker is later revealed as Captain Angel operating under deception. The advice therefore remains testimony from a manipulator, not privileged authorial truth.
- Enterprise is captured through a staged humanitarian narrative and pirate boarding operation. Pike and Una then exploit divisions within the pirate crew, using food, status anxiety, and a fabricated sales plan to provoke mutiny.
- Angel reveals that the real Dr. Aspen was abandoned alive, that the endangered colonists were invented, and that the operation's actual objective was Spock. Earlier Aspen biography and distress claims are therefore explicitly disconfirmed/deceptive evidence.
- Angel uses Spock as leverage against T'Pring to obtain a Vulcan called Xaverius. Spock and Chapel stage a romantic betrayal and Spock/T'Pring stage a ritual severing as a counter-deception. T'Pring later states that she recognized the ruse, and the bond is restored.
- Angel explicitly claims to have manipulated the crew through stories and emotion while continuing to challenge Spock's identity categories. The episode thereby supplies counterevidence against reading Angel's earlier counseling persona as a neutral therapeutic authority.
- The final identification of Xaverius with Spock's half-brother Sybok is delivered as Spock's inference/explanation after Angel escapes. Preserve that epistemic route rather than pretending the transcript supplied an omniscient identity merge.

## Cross-episode frame and counterevidence traps

- **E01:** a remembered future event motivates present action, but memory/vision is not automatically immutable fate; institutional noninterference is challenged by prior contamination.
- **E02:** the Shepherds' cosmology and the comet's apparent anticipatory message remain unresolved rather than being forced into either supernatural certainty or dismissive coincidence.
- **E04:** traumatic memory, mind-meld access, tactical expertise, and species-essentialist testimony must remain distinct evidence classes.
- **E06:** ceremonial claims of free choice coexist with childhood conditioning, concealed material consequences, political necessity claims, and a parent's attempted rescue; `consent` should not be coded from the ritual formula alone.
- **E07:** a deceptive persona delivers thematically resonant identity advice; later revelation changes source credibility without erasing the fact that the statements were made and affected Spock.
