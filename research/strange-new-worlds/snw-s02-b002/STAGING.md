## Neutral close-read staging notes

### S02E05 — Charades

- A shuttle accident involving a Kerkhovian transport tunnel injures Spock. The Kerkhovians describe their intervention as a remediation based on mismatched biological instructions; the resulting Spock is depicted as biologically human rather than his prior mixed human/Vulcan biological state.
- The Kerkhovians' description of what counted as a successful remediation is their technical/administrative framing. Their action changes a biological continuity dimension but does not by itself establish a new legal, social, memory, causal, or global personal identity.
- Spock retains memories, relationships, role, and self-reference while experiencing changed physiology and emotional regulation. These dimensions should remain distinct rather than converting the temporary biological change into a binary same-person/different-person judgment.
- The episode explicitly states through its characters that Vulcans have emotions and practice suppression/control. Spock's changed reactions therefore should not be coded as proof that emotion is uniquely human or absent from Vulcans.
- Spock and his allies conceal his altered biology during a Vulcan engagement ritual. The successful performance separates social presentation and institutional recognition from current biological state.
- Amanda and other participants describe legal/familial consequences that could follow failure of the engagement ritual. Those statements are testimony about Vulcan social and legal norms unless separately supported by stronger evidence.
- T'Pril repeatedly characterizes Spock's human ancestry in derogatory terms. Her statements establish prejudice and evaluative belief, not an objective deficit in Spock or humans.
- Chapel challenges the Kerkhovian remediation and obtains material that restores Spock's Vulcan genome. Her argument that the altered Spock is not fully himself is Chapel's relational/identity interpretation; the directly depicted evidence is the biological alteration and later restoration.
- Spock eventually discloses that he completed the ritual while biologically human and rejects T'Pril's characterization of his human heritage as a handicap. This supplies direct counterevidence to the ritual participants' earlier assumptions about what a human could accomplish.
- T'Pring responds primarily to Spock's decision not to trust her with the truth while involving many others in the deception; she asks for time apart. The relationship consequence is therefore not reducible to Spock's biology.
- After restoration, Spock states that he remains conflicted and has feelings for someone else. The episode ends with Spock and Chapel acting on that attraction. Biological restoration does not simply restore the prior interpersonal state.

### S02E06 — Lost in Translation

- Uhura experiences an unusual auditory signal and visions while exhausted near a deuterium-refining project. Initial medical evidence supports exhaustion and mild deuterium exposure as a plausible cause.
- Uhura notes that she perceived the signal before the exposure used to explain it. A second affected person, Saul Ramon, later presents a closely related syndrome. These are explicit counterevidence against treating the first medical explanation as sufficient.
- Hemmer appears within Uhura's visions. The episode later frames the visual material as her mind interpreting an unfamiliar communication through memories and associations; it does not depict Hemmer as objectively returned or physically present.
- Ramon records his own experiences and acts on his interpretation of them. His logs are documentary/self-report evidence and should remain distinct from the later explanation of the phenomenon.
- Uhura describes having imagined details of the accident that killed her family despite not witnessing it. Those imagined scenes are evidence about grief and mental representation, not direct evidence of the historical crash.
- Uhura and Kirk assemble a pattern connecting the signal, language-center effects, Ramon's condition, deuterium, and the refinery. Uhura interprets the phenomenon as communication from life forms being harmed by the refining operation.
- Pike explicitly asks Uhura about her certainty before acting. The refinery is evacuated and destroyed, after which the signal/experience ceases and Uhura reports that the beings are safe. The operational outcome strengthens the communication/harm interpretation without turning every intermediate hypothesis into omniscient fact.
- Pelia violates an order while investigating sabotage and later argues that Una's hostility toward her is connected to grief over Hemmer and Pelia's replacement of him. Pelia's psychological explanation is interpretation/testimony, even where Una's behavior supplies contextual support.
- La'An's interaction with James Kirk invokes her knowledge of another temporal experience that this Kirk does not share. The episode preserves asymmetric memory/relationship history rather than treating two temporal encounters as interchangeable.

### S02E07 — Those Old Scientists

- Boimler arrives from roughly 120 years in the future through an ancient portal. The crew explicitly treats his knowledge and attachments as potential temporal contamination rather than privileged omniscient information.
- Boimler's statements about Enterprise personnel repeatedly reflect later historical reputation and his own fandom. They are evidence of 24th-century historical understanding and personal expectations, not automatic evidence that the 23rd-century people currently possess those traits or destinies.
- His expectations materially affect the people he meets. Chapel is disturbed by information about the future Spock; Uhura feels pressure from learning how widely admired her later career becomes; Una reacts to later Starfleet remembrance. Future knowledge therefore becomes a causal event in the present rather than remaining passive metadata.
- Boimler initially assumes an approaching Orion vessel is peaceful partly because of future cultural knowledge. The D'Var is indeed a science vessel, but its crew also takes the portal and initially conceals doing so. The episode therefore resists both `all Orions are pirates` and an opposite simplistic innocence stereotype.
- Boimler reveals that his Orion friend Tendi has a great-grandmother aboard D'Var. He repeatedly worries that present violence could erase or alter people in his future, making causal continuity relevant without proving what changes would necessarily occur.
- Mariner follows Boimler through the portal, consuming the remaining horonium and creating a second temporal-displacement problem. Their attempts to help also worsen the immediate grain shortage and produce further historical intervention.
- Boimler recognizes that imposing his later expectations on the current crew denies their present reality. Spock independently reasons that allowing future reputation to dictate his current emotional choices would itself alter the path that produced that future.
- Historical knowledge about NX-class construction ultimately supplies the physical horonium needed to reactivate the portal. Future historical records therefore both endanger and enable restoration of the temporal situation.
- At the end, Pike explicitly negotiates with Captain Caras so that history will credit Orion scientists with discovery of the portal. In the 24th century Tendi's prior claim is then presented as correct. This is a direct example of historical record being shaped by an in-episode agreement; later history should not be treated as an independent camera on the original event.
- The returned travelers' later discussion of the experience is itself downstream of the intervention. Cross-era references should preserve which temporal vantage supplied each claim.

### S02E09 — Subspace Rhapsody

- A communication experiment involving a naturally occurring subspace fold and a musical transmission produces an improbability field in which people spontaneously sing. Spock's explanation is a scientific hypothesis developed from observed effects and simulations, then revised as additional behavior appears.
- Singing is involuntary or externally compelled enough that characters repeatedly describe inability to prevent it. The musical state therefore constitutes an altered-reality evidentiary frame.
- Characters disclose private feelings while singing. The episode explicitly identifies this forced disclosure as a security risk. A sung statement establishes that the character expressed it under the field; it should not automatically be promoted to an unqualified, context-free proposition about stable beliefs.
- Some disclosures are subsequently addressed in ordinary conversation, which provides independent support for their interpersonal significance. Pike and Batel discuss the avoidance revealed during their musical exchange; La'An tells Kirk about her alternate-timeline relationship; Chapel and Spock confront the consequences of her fellowship decision and their relationship.
- The field spreads through the subspace communications network to other Federation ships and later affects Klingons. The expansion creates institutional and military consequences beyond Enterprise.
- An attempted technical closure fails, and testing shows that a direct destructive strike could propagate catastrophically through the affected network. This explicitly disconfirms the first closure plan.
- Uhura identifies a correlation between musical events and measurable increases in the improbability field. The crew deliberately generates a large communal musical event to exceed the modeled threshold and collapse the field.
- The post-event log states that Federation vessels have returned to normal behavior, while diplomacy is required with the affected Klingons. This supplies an in-universe operational resolution without making every emotion disclosed under compulsion permanently authoritative.
- La'An later says she is glad she told Kirk the truth despite the painful outcome. That voluntary post-field assessment should be distinguished from disclosures produced directly by the musical compulsion.

### S02E10 — Hegemony

- Enterprise encounters a Gorn attack around Parnassus Beta while Starfleet establishes a demarcation line and orders restraint. Pike chooses to cross the line with a volunteer landing party to seek survivors, creating an explicit conflict between rescue goals and escalation constraints.
- Previously ineffective detection and combat methods have been modified. Characters discuss revised tricorder detection, weapons, and tactical procedures as learned responses to prior Gorn encounters.
- Gorn forces use coordinated ships, an interference system, a demarcation response, and recognition/transponder-like technology. On the surface, young Gorn are observed cooperating rather than fighting for dominance as La'An expected. These depictions are counterevidence to reducing the Gorn to mindless or behaviorally uniform predators.
- La'An's prior model of Gorn behavior is treated as valuable expertise but not infallible. Pike explicitly notes that unexpected cooperation means there is more to learn.
- Montgomery Scott reports that Gorn appeared after an unusual series of coronal mass ejections near the Stardiver's assignment. Sam Kirk proposes that certain solar activity might trigger a swarming/consumption cycle. The episode treats this as potentially critical intelligence and a hypothesis to investigate, not a settled biological fact.
- Scott reports reverse-engineering enough of Gorn scanning/recognition behavior to build a device that made his shuttle appear Gorn to scanners. This supports technological adaptability and a specific evasion mechanism without proving complete knowledge of Gorn systems.
- Spock acts on the explicit uncertainty that Chapel's fate is not known, even after Cayuga's sickbay appears destroyed. Chapel is subsequently depicted alive, providing direct counterevidence to an earlier reasonable but incomplete inference of death.
- The Enterprise plan uses the Cayuga saucer's apparent orbital decay to destroy the Gorn interference source without firing directly across the demarcation line. The source's destruction restores communications and transport capability.
- Batel reveals she has been implanted with Gorn eggs and initially proposes sacrificing herself rather than risking others. Pike refuses to treat that as the only available outcome; aboard Enterprise, Batel asks Chapel to kill her if the condition cannot be stopped, while Chapel explicitly refuses to give up on treatment.
- Before Enterprise can retrieve the surface party and colonists, sensors show non-Starfleet transport signatures and no human life signs remaining on the planet. The crew infers that the Gorn beamed them away. This is a sensor-supported inference, not direct depiction of their destination or later condition.
- Starfleet then orders Enterprise to withdraw while its people remain in Gorn custody. The episode ends before Pike answers the command dilemma. The correct staging status for that conflict is unresolved, not an invented resolution imported from later material.

## Cross-episode frame and counterevidence traps

- **E05:** a major biological alteration occurs without corresponding loss of memory, social role, relationships, or self-reference; restoration of biology does not restore the prior relationship state.
- **E06:** a plausible medical explanation is weakened by timing and a second case; visions use memory imagery as a communication vocabulary rather than proving the remembered/dead figure is present.
- **E07:** later historical reputation changes present behavior, and the episode explicitly shows an agreement shaping what later history records about discovery.
- **E09:** compelled musical disclosure occurs inside an altered-reality frame; later voluntary conversations provide separate evidence where characters adopt, reject, or act on what was disclosed.
- **E10:** accumulated expertise about the Gorn remains revisable; multiple observations contradict species-essentialist assumptions, while the proposed solar-trigger mechanism remains a hypothesis at episode end.
