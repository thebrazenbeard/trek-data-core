## Neutral close-read staging notes

### S04E02 — Griffin Incident

- The USS Griffin disappeared nine years earlier with approximately 300 people aboard and is found intact with unexpected gravity/power but no detected life signs. Earlier Klingon and stellar-event explanations had failed; the episode begins from an unresolved historical mystery rather than a settled cause.
- Spock, La'An, and James Kirk board. An apparent Rose Harper is encountered despite the absence of life signs and then disappears. The manifestation leaves anomalous evidence but no ordinary transporter residue. `Historical Harper` and `current apparent Harper` should remain separate local occurrences unless later reconciliation establishes more.
- Griffin records describe shipwide erratic behavior, violence, self-harm, and delusions while medical examinations failed to establish an ordinary cause. Historical Harper was relieved/confined after reporting that something was taking over the crew. Computer records attribute many deaths to violence and to people being transported into open space alive. Those records are in-universe documentary evidence rather than omniscient narration.
- The ship/manipulating phenomenon presents figures and voices tied to private memories and fears. Spock encounters an apparent T'Pring, Kirk encounters material connected to family/fatherhood/death anxieties, and La'An encounters Harper/identity-related pressure.
- The apparent T'Pring has access to intimate material, but Spock explicitly reasons that private-memory fidelity does not prove she is the actual T'Pring. He later treats the apparition as a manipulation built from his own memories and beliefs. This is a strong regression case against `knows private facts -> SAME_AS` identity reasoning.
- Manipulated perceptions produce **real material actions**. Enterprise personnel under the influence attempt dangerous acts against themselves or others. That does not make induced statements transparent evidence of stable preferences or ordinary voluntary intention.
- Uhura attempts a transporter action that would catastrophically fuse/overlap people; La'An attacks Kirk; Una and Pike voice identity/life alternatives under the phenomenon's influence. These acts/utterances belong to an altered-cognition/manipulation frame and must remain distinct from later voluntary evaluations.
- The phenomenon repeatedly frames destructive release as freedom or a character's `true self`. That is the manipulator's ideological presentation, not source-certified psychology.
- Enterprise removes the affected personnel and survives the incident. The Griffin is transferred to Division 12. Pike states that several previous Division 12 cases eventually received scientific or alien explanations while some remained unresolved; this incident is not given a final mechanism in the episode.
- La'An notes that the official report omits her attempt to kill Kirk. Pike accepts a narrowed report focused on survival. The institutional record is therefore knowingly incomplete relative to depicted events and should never be used as if it were the sole transparent account.
- Kirk's experience leads him to initiate contact regarding Carol/David. The episode supports that behavioral consequence; it does not establish any supernatural prophecy about his future.

### S04E03 — Human Best Friend

- Enterprise is en route to the Rodaxian system for peace negotiations while Alpha shift is visibly overworked. Ortegas and Scotty open with personal logs that also establish ongoing friction between them. Una argues that the shift needs a break.
- Cesnar Grand is presented as a technology-free shore-leave resort whose naturally intoxicating atmosphere produces individualized experiences intended to help visitors relax. This is partly resort marketing/testimony and partly supported by the crew's directly depicted altered states.
- The resort's terms state that atmospheric effects should wear off after departure and that arrival constitutes acceptance of liability terms. Later Dendru points to fine/microprint when crew members complain about hangovers and missing time. Contractual disclosure, marketing promise, and actual physiological outcome are therefore different evidentiary layers.
- Dendru says retained negative emotions can negate or alter the atmospheric effects. That explanation is resort-representative testimony unless independently supported; it should not be treated as a universal psychological mechanism merely because it is narratively useful.
- Crew behavior on the planet occurs under an **altered-physiological/altered-cognition frame**. Intoxicated acts and statements establish that the acts/statements occurred, but do not automatically establish durable preferences, beliefs, or ordinary voluntary disclosures.
- Examples of altered conduct include unusually uninhibited, impulsive, recreational, confrontational, or identity-experimenting behavior across Alpha shift. Those scenes are valuable as altered-state evidence but poor candidates for flattening into stable character traits.
- Some crew later experience hangovers and substantial missing time. The need to reconstruct events from partial memories, other people's statements, physical traces, and subsequent evidence creates an explicit epistemic gap.
- An urgent mission message cuts the leave short. Ortegas and Scotty discover the shuttle is not where they expect and must reconstruct their prior actions while still in conflict with one another.
- Their recovery/repair effort requires each to confront limits in the other's specialty and to cooperate. The episode supports a change in their immediate working relationship without erasing the prior conflict or proving a permanent personality change.
- The `personalized relaxation` promise is therefore counterbalanced by incomplete consent/disclosure, variable physiological effects, missing memory, and behavior the crew would not necessarily endorse in an ordinary frame.

### S04E04 — A Case of Chiaroscuro

- Una, M'Benga, and Uhura are assigned to recover the malfunctioning Federation probe Angelo-1 after it drifts into Klingon-controlled space near occupied Mat'Aar 12. A Klingon vessel prevents an easy return route, and the away team lands/blends with the local population rather than escalating immediately.
- The occupation is evidenced through identification requirements, search-and-seizure threats, propaganda/surveillance, collaborator armbands, and the local collaborator organization. M'Benga confidently describes the social pattern, then acknowledges he has personally visited zero Klingon-occupied planets. His inference may be useful, but expertise/war history is not the same as direct local experience.
- Roman sees Una and identifies her as **Nevette**, his missing boss, whom he has not heard from for two days and feared dead. Una then adopts the mistaken identity to protect the mission and presents M'Benga/Uhura as new recruits.
- Una and Nevette must remain distinct local entities. The source initially establishes physical resemblance plus social misidentification, not biological relation, duplication, timeline identity, or any other global equivalence.
- The collaborator organization has detained a suspected resistance member and seeks the identity of an accomplice before a Klingon commander arrives. Una is expected to perform the interrogation while impersonating Nevette. Statements addressed to `Nevette` during this sequence are evidence about local beliefs and Nevette's reputation/history, not Una's biography.
- Klingon authority explicitly privileges brutality over information and treats resistance activity as treason. This is an institutional/occupational position expressed by the occupier, not neutral moral classification of the resistance.
- Later information establishes that Nevette's public collaborator position concealed a role in the local resistance. `Collaborator` and `resistance leader` are therefore not mutually exclusive identity labels in this case; the public role functions inside a covert strategy.
- Roman is implicated in Nevette's disappearance/death while Una's resemblance lets the resistance/collaboration network temporarily treat her as if Nevette had returned. The impersonation becomes causally important without changing Una's actual identity.
- The episode's noir framing and unusual local visual conditions are form/environmental presentation; genre convention does not suspend normal evidence rules.
- Una's new command status and concern about whether her moral compass is sufficient are established before the undercover crisis. Decisions under the Nevette identity therefore provide evidence about Una's command problem-solving, but local praise/blame aimed at Nevette must not be silently reassigned to Una.
- The episode repeatedly destabilizes clean binaries: collaborator/resister, law/criminality, occupation/order, public/private identity, and moral certainty. Those tensions are source-supported without requiring this worker to resolve them globally.

## Cross-episode frame and counterevidence traps

- **S04E02:** a manifestation can possess private-memory fidelity without being the remembered person; induced cognition can generate real harmful actions while still making utterances unreliable as stable preference evidence; official reports can knowingly omit depicted events.
- **S04E03:** intoxicated/altered-state behavior and missing-memory reconstruction must not be mined as ordinary voluntary personality evidence; resort marketing, fine print, representative explanation, and actual outcome are separate evidentiary layers.
- **S04E04:** physical resemblance and assumed identity cause local social projection without establishing global sameness; Nevette's collaborator and resistance roles coexist rather than forcing a single binary allegiance label.
